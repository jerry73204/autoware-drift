# Seed both valleys, let IPOPT refine each

(Research report, 2026-10-30: three literature tracks, notes in `mode-choice-notes/`, grounded in `drift-optimality.md`. Citations tagged [verified] were checked against a source this session; [unverified] ones were not.)

**Recommendation: choose grip or drift by solving L1's window from two or three mode seeds in parallel on CPU cores and keeping the fastest feasible plan, with a hysteresis gate that defaults to grip.** Every published real-time racing stack that faces a discrete choice inside a non-convex problem does it this way: one solve per class, keep the cheapest. None of them hands the choice to the NLP. Our own study bounds what the choice is worth. On asphalt drift wins only in tight hairpins and by at most 0.4 %, so the gate should keep the car on grip there. On low friction drift is worth 1-2 % and changes the line from about 25 m before the corner, so it has to be decided early in the window. Of the 20 mu 0.45 cases, 8 are undecided because a warm start never leaves its own valley ([drift-optimality.md](drift-optimality.md)). A GPU does not help the IPOPT solve or the HPIPM tracker: at roughly 1000 variables the CPU is 5-14x faster than the best GPU interior-point stack. It earns a place only in front of IPOPT, as a batched rollout sampler that proposes which valleys to seed. Build that only if fixed seeds turn out to miss valleys. Continuation belongs offline, as a way to build seeds. Mixed-integer and complementarity formulations are too slow for a 1 s replan. A learned classifier trained on the study's own labels is a cheap later addition. The plan below tests this offline first, then closed loop on `sim.Plant`, then in Isaac. Each stage has metrics and pass criteria.

## The ridge is a half-slide, so the gradient cannot see across it

The first question is why the derivative solver cannot choose. Nothing in L1 is discrete. Rear slip angle, steering and acceleration request are all continuous, and no variable says "drift". The non-convexity comes from the tyre: force rises to a peak and falls past it. So window time has separate valleys (grip, rear stepped out at turn-in, a pendulum flick), and between them lies a ridge. On that ridge sits a half-slide, with the rear 1-3 deg past its peak, that loses grip without buying rotation and is slower than either valley. IPOPT's Newton steps only go downhill from the start, and live warm-starting from the shifted plan keeps the solver in the valley it is already in ([drift-optimality.md](drift-optimality.md), "Why differentiation cannot choose"). Some of the choice is genuinely discrete even in a smooth model: which way the car rotates (a flick has a zero-slide ridge between its two directions), and whether the slide starts on the brakes or on the power.

The study also shows what the choice is worth, and that sets the design. **On asphalt (mu 1.12, sliding 0.8) drift wins only in 180 deg hairpins of R ≤ 25 m, by at most 0.4 % (0.03 s), and on 90 deg corners never.** At mu 0.3, drift wins in nearly every corner, by up to 1.6-1.8 % (0.19-0.28 s) with no fall-off. The 170 deg study shows *what* a drift buys. On asphalt the drift line is 0.1-0.2 m/s slower at its slowest point and exits at the same speed. It wins by finishing the rotation 0.06-0.21 s sooner, at a cost of 2-15 % more tyre heat. On snow the held plan ploughs the front, so the drift is a little faster, but its currency is still earlier rotation. The study's solver noise (the spread over starts) reaches **0.28 s on mu 0.45 hairpins**, which is why 8 of 20 cases there stay undecided ([drift-optimality.md](drift-optimality.md)). Two consequences follow. On asphalt the two valleys are about as far apart as solver tolerance and model error, so any chooser without hysteresis will chatter between modes. On low friction the gain is real, but the planner's tyre peaks at 3 deg on snow where Isaac's peaks at 6 deg, so the low-friction gains are probably optimistic until that is fixed.

The literature points the same way. Tavernini et al. find low-drift solutions optimal on paved surfaces and high-drift, counter-steering ones on loose surfaces, depending on drivetrain ([Surrey](https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Minimum-time-cornering-the-effect-of/99640964802346)) [verified, abstract]. The handbrake study finds the handbrake turn to be the minimum-time hairpin technique on loose ground. But it needed a "proper yet simple cost function" before the optimiser would use it, which is circumstantial evidence that the drift basin is small ([Southampton](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/382093/)) [verified, search summary; full text 403]. A TU Delft thesis on hairpin minimum-time manoeuvring names convergence "to various local minima" as the main limit on precision ([TU Delft](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:c3a0dfec-530e-4672-b4cd-55374817e686)) [verified, abstract]. A Lund paper says online time-optimal generation is "still not there, mainly because of the inherent nonconvexity" ([Lund PDF](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/4190779/4015826.pdf)) [verified, snippet]. **No paper found reports a grip-versus-drift time gap as a percentage. Ours may be the only such figures.**

## Racing stacks enumerate discrete choices before the solver, never inside it

No real-time stack reviewed chooses a driving technique online. Every one that faces a discrete choice (which side to overtake, which corridor to take) does the same thing: it enumerates the classes upstream and refines each one locally. He, Zeng and Sreenath solve one MPC per homotopic Bézier reference between opponents, in parallel, and select "the time-optimal trajectory among these different homotopic trajectories" ([arXiv:2112.06435](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.06435)) [verified, abstract]. That is seeded multi-start with a minimum-time rule, our exact design with homotopy classes in place of modes. Reiter et al. select a homotopy class by mixed-integer optimisation first and only then solve the continuous OCP ([PDF](https://publications.syscop.de/Reiter2021a.pdf)) [verified, structure; timings not extracted]. Liniger et al. put a dynamic-programming corridor chooser in front of a convex QP at 50 Hz with millisecond solves ([arXiv:1711.07300](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07300)) [verified, abstract]. A 2026 overtaking paper removed its homotopy-selection layer in an ablation, and success rates dropped because the continuous MPC then had to resolve the side choice inside a non-convex region ([arXiv:2607.13348](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.13348)) [verified, snippet; authors not retrieved]. **That ablation is the closest published evidence that leaving the choice to the NLP is worse.**

The drift-control literature tracks drifts; it does not choose them. The Stanford/TRI line, from MARTY's equilibrium tracking ([Stanford DDL](https://ddl.stanford.edu/publications/conference-proceeding/simultaneous-stabilization-and-tracking-basic-automobile)) [verified] to the GR Supra NMPC that "can smoothly transition from dynamic, non-equilibrium drifting to grip driving" ([Toyota pressroom](https://pressroom.toyota.com/toyota-research-institute-pushes-vehicles-capabilities-to-advance-active-safety/)) [verified], is given the drift as a task: a sideslip reference, or "drift around the obstacle". It never treats the drift as a lap-time decision. Hierarchical cornering frameworks place the drift segment by geometry (entry, drift, exit) ([IEEE 10588721](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10588721/)) [verified, abstract]. Our map argues against a geometric rule, because on asphalt it would drift corners that grip takes as fast or faster. The only academic work that poses grip versus drift as a decision is Ajanović et al., who treat it as task-and-motion planning: a search over mode-specific motion primitives, where the mode choice depends on whether the continuous motion in each mode is feasible ([arXiv:2301.10384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10384)) [verified, abstract; no timings]. Transferable from this line: the split in which a slow planner chooses the mode and a fast tracker serves both regimes, which TRI (about 20 Hz replanning) and TUM (100 Hz drift tracking with a 170 ms delay predictor, [arXiv:2606.27914](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.27914)) [verified] both use. Our L1/L2 split already has that shape.

The candidates line up as follows for a 1 s L1 replan on a ~1000-variable window.

Three points matter for the design. **First, continuation reaches a valley but does not choose one.** Cots uses differential path-following to converge families of minimum-time vehicle OCPs ([arXiv:1903.04048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.04048)) [verified], and no paper found runs friction continuation inside a real-time loop. Its place is building the drift seed: solve with no fall-off, where drift wins easily, then step to the surface's fall-off. A receding warm start is itself continuation in time. Once a drift seed converges, later replans stay in that valley by the same mechanism that now traps the planner in grip. **Second, a sampler's weighted average is dangerous on a bimodal problem.** MPPI's softmin average over rollouts from two valleys can land on the ridge, which is exactly the half-slide our model penalises. Any sampler must therefore pick per-mode clusters or argmin, not the mean (an inference in the notes, consistent with diffusion-MPC work finding that L2 regression averages modes, [arXiv:2504.04603](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04603v3)) [verified, snippet]. **Third, smoothing and complementarity remain local methods.** An MPCC relaxed and solved by IPOPT still stays in its start's valley. Only branch-and-bound decides exactly, and on a 1000-variable non-convex OCP that is far outside 1 s (an inference; the notes found no MINLP lap-time timings). Convex lap-time formulations get global optimality only by dropping the sideslip non-convexity that creates the drift valley ([arXiv:2111.04650](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.04650v1)) [verified, snippet].

## A GPU belongs in front of IPOPT, not under it

The second question is whether a GPU helps. **For one ~1000-variable banded NLP, the GPU is slower.** The cleanest benchmark is MadNLP + cuDSS against MadNLP + MUMPS on optimal-control problems. At N=100 grid points the CPU is 11x faster on Goddard (0.0146 s against 0.158 s on an A100) and 14x faster on an H100. The GPU overtakes only above N≈2000-5000 on Goddard and N≈500 on a quadrotor ([arXiv:2510.03932](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03932v2)) [verified, full tables]. Our 50-65 stages sit at the CPU-winning end. The large GPU speedups reported (about 18x for MadNLP on AC-OPF case70k) come from problems with tens of thousands of buses ([arXiv:2403.15913](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.15913v2)) [verified, snippet]. L2 is no different: ReLU-QP on GPU is only competitive with CPU solvers on small and medium QPs ([arXiv:2311.18056](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.18056)) [verified, abstract], and HPIPM's Riccati QP solves a chain-of-masses benchmark in under 1 ms ([arXiv:2003.02547](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.02547)) [verified, snippet]. Per-solve speed should therefore come from structure: a Riccati or HSL linear solver in place of MUMPS, fewer iterations, a dedicated core. The claim that MA27/MA57 beat MUMPS on banded problems is unquantified in the notes [unverified], so it is an experiment below, not a promise.

Batched multi-start on the GPU pays only at batch sizes our problem does not need. WarpMPC's batched SQP beats its baselines only above about 2000 instances, and a dense batched OSQP showed no throughput gain over one CPU core at small batch ([arXiv:2607.11603](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11603)) [verified, snippet]. jaxipm, the first GPU-batched IPOPT-style solver, is a 2026 preprint with quadrotor benchmarks only ([arXiv:2606.26341](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26341)) [verified, abstract]. With 2-3 seeds, the host's 32 cores beat any of these.

The GPU is the right tool for the step that derivatives do badly: proposing valleys. MPPI-Generic runs 2048 rollouts of 100 steps in **0.146 ms** on an RTX 4090 with a simple differential-drive model ([arXiv:2409.07563](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.07563v4)) [verified, benchmark section]. AutoRally ran about 4.8 million full nonlinear vehicle-dynamics evaluations per second on a 2014 GTX 750 Ti ([arXiv:1707.02342](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.02342.pdf)) [verified, full text]. The notes estimate that a two-track combined-slip model costs 10-100x the arithmetic of the diff-drive step [unverified estimate]. Even at 100x that is about 15 ms per probe, well inside a 1 s replan on this host's RTX 5090. CusADi compiles CasADi functions to CUDA for thousands of batched evaluations, so L1's existing `node_functions` could be rolled out on the GPU without writing a second model ([arXiv:2408.09662](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.09662)) [verified, snippet]. The limits are just as clear from AutoRally's full text. Horizons were 2-3 s, not our 8-20 s window. Track limits were soft penalties, and without a time decay on them the real car destabilised. Sideslip was capped by cost at 0.75 rad. CEM was faster but completed only 66 % of laps at 11 m/s, "unable to finely discriminate between trajectories which do and do not contact the barrier" ([arXiv:1707.02342](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.02342.pdf)) [verified, full text]. So a sampler on our car is a **corner-local probe over a few mode parameters** (slide direction, onset point, peak rear slip past the peak), scored by time to a gate past the corner. It hands the best 2-3 distinct clusters to IPOPT and never drives the car itself. The hybrid pattern is well supported outside racing: CEM-GD matched CEM with 100x fewer samples ([arXiv:2112.07746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07746)) [verified, abstract], and cuRobo's parallel seeds plus L-BFGS run about 60x faster than prior trajectory optimisers ([arXiv:2310.17274](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17274v2)) [verified, abstract]. **No published racing or drift work reports gains from sampling a seed for an NLP**, so this is an open design point, not established practice.

## Recommendation: three seeds, a grip-default gate, then measure before adding a GPU

The design follows from all of the above. At each replan, L1 solves the window from **seed G**, the shifted previous plan as today, and **seed D**, a rear-out seed. The study suggests where D can come from: the free plan of the nearest stored corner archetype (radius × turn × mu × fall-off), solved offline by fall-off continuation and mapped into the window's curvilinear coordinates. A synthetic profile is the fallback: the rear held 5-10 deg past the tyre's peak from 25 m before turn-in to the apex, with braking on low mu and coasting on asphalt. A third, optional **seed F** is a pendulum flick, rotated the other way first. Each seed runs in its own process with its own compiled `Window` and `budget` (IPOPT's `max_wall_time`) of about 0.9 s. Any seed not feasible by then is dropped, which the window already allows ([window.py](../src/planning/race_planner/race_planner/window.py)). The selection rule is minimum window time among feasible plans, with **hysteresis**: switch away from the current mode only when the alternative is faster by more than max(noise floor, a threshold of about 0.3 % of window time) on two consecutive replans, and require a drift plan's corner to be at least about 25 m ahead so its trail-braking phase is not cut short. That gate keeps asphalt on grip except in hairpins where the gain clears it, matching the evidence that a 0.4 % gain is smaller than the tracking error a real drift controller adds (TUM: 1.1 m lateral, 0.06 rad sideslip overshoot ([arXiv:2606.27914](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.27914)) [verified]).

Two budget facts constrain this. The 0.5 s figure is the five-control form. `window.py`'s own measurements put the seven-control window at **p95 0.91 s and p99 1.04 s** on Haruna. A cold-ish drift seed will take more iterations than a warm one, so overruns of the 1 s budget are the expected failure mode. The cheaper fallbacks are to run seed D only when the window holds a corner below a radius or friction threshold, or only every second replan. `live.Driver` also assumes one solver (`busy_until`), so a multi-start driver needs a process pool and its own adoption logic. Roadmap 060 flags this risk: "the multi-start may cost more cores than the budget allows" ([060-live-race-planner.md](https://github.com/NEWSLabNTU/scenario-world-bridge/blob/main/docs/roadmap/060-live-race-planner.md)). Before trusting any low-friction gain, fix the planner's per-surface tyre peak and add the open-axle arm, both caveats in the study.

## Experiment plan: offline oracle, receding chain, sim.Plant, Isaac

The order matters: each step answers whether the next one is worth building. All of it uses existing code: `studies/drift_optimality.py` (section builder, `Window(beta_max, rear_alpha_max)`, `good()`), `live.Driver`/`live.run`, `sim.Plant`, and swb's `course_probe` for Isaac.

Read E0 first. If {G,D} already reaches the oracle and fits 0.9 s, E3 is not needed and the GPU stays idle on this problem. If the D seed's solve time is the problem, E0b and the "D only near tight or slippery corners" schedule come before more hardware. If E2 shows the gain on low friction but E4 does not, the cause is the model's tyre rather than the chooser, and that points back to the caveats, not to a better mode selector.

## Conclusion

The grip-or-drift problem is not a solver problem but a seeding problem. An exact local refiner already exists (IPOPT on L1). What it lacks is a starting point in each valley, and the cheapest reliable supply of those is a handful of mode seeds on spare CPU cores. The literature offers no more powerful real-time alternative. It shows that the field moves discrete choices upstream of the solver, and that nobody has yet measured what a drift is worth. Our own map says the answer is "almost nothing on asphalt". That turns the hysteresis gate, not the seed search, into the safety-critical part of the design. A chooser that flips on 0.4 % will cost more in tracking than it gains.

The GPU question resolves into a measurement rather than a purchase. The RTX 5090 is the right tool only if E0 shows that hand-written and continuation seeds miss valleys the oracle finds. Even then it proposes seeds and never optimises the plan. The most informative single result to get next is E0's coverage number: the share of oracle-optimal drift plans that a grip seed plus one rear-out seed recovers within the live budget.
