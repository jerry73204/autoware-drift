# Let the map pick seeds, let evidence commit

(Research report, 2026-10-30: three literature tracks, notes in `preconditions-notes/`, grounded in roadmap 061 M3b, 060 M6 and `drift-optimality.md`. Citations tagged [verified] were checked against a source this session; [unverified] ones were not.)

Real racing teams choose strategy from preconditions in two layers. First, a cheap and generous prior decides which options stay open. Then live measurement, compared against a crossover, decides which option they commit to. The costly failures in the notes come from under-reacting to a precondition that was getting worse, not from carrying an extra option. For roadmap 061 M3b this means three things. A drift (D) or flick (F) seed should be added whenever the window ahead *plausibly* holds low friction, a wet hairpin or a tight hairpin, using the map's surface class with its uncertainty. A drift plan should be adopted only on the existing strict gate (0.3 % of the window's time, two re-plans in a row, grip on a tie). Onboard evidence may only ever *downgrade* the surface. The second finding is just as firm. **No onboard sensor in the literature supplies friction 200-260 m ahead.** Published camera surface classifiers stop at 30 m. Slip-, steering-torque- and tyre-based estimators see 0 m ahead by construction. No sensing method found measures fall-off past the peak at all. Over the planning window, peak friction and fall-off must therefore come from the map's surface class. Perception can only confirm or refute that class within roughly 30-60 m, and the car's own estimator can only correct the map after the car has driven the segment. Geometry, grade and width also come from the map. In our Isaac worlds they are exact by construction, and on real roads their errors are known and large enough to matter for the R 9 m against R 25 m split. Because swb owns the ground truth, the right sim experiment is a planner fed a *deliberately wrong* map and a *simulated* perception sensor whose misclassification rate grows with distance. Rendering pixels and training a classifier is not needed for this.

## F1 switches tyres on a measured crossover, and loses races on asymmetric risk

In F1 the trigger for changing tyres is a performance proxy measured against a reference, not the weather variable itself. Pirelli's rule of thumb is to move from slicks to intermediates once lap times reach **110-112 % of the dry slick lap**, for example about 1:40 against a 1:30 dry lap. A similar percentage step marks the move from inters to full wets. Pirelli gives no water-depth threshold, although it quantifies water clearance (about 35-40 L/s per inter at 300 km/h) ([Pirelli](https://www.pirelli.com/global/en-ww/race/when-it-s-time-to-change-from-slick-to-wet-tyres-in-formula-1)). The crossover exists because near it both tyres are degraded: inters overheat as the track dries while slicks cannot reach temperature ([Ferrari Tech Talk](https://www.ferrari.com/en-CZ/formula1/articles/tech-talk-turkish-grand-prix-2020)). The crossover also depends on circuit and season. Pirelli put Suzuka 2022's inter-to-slick crossover at 1:40 and noted that new tyres that year moved the crossover points ([Pirelli press](https://press.pirelli.com/2022-japanese-grand-prix---friday/)). When the moment comes, teams trust a live probe over a forecast. At Suzuka 2022, sector times from one driver's first flying lap on the other tyre showed the inter about 4 s faster than the wet, and that lap was judged the moment to stop ([F1.com](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/monday-morning-debrief-why-nailing-the-strategy-was-so-tough-at-the-rain.6mVFnAE6LiMq5jK9IjN0gy)).

The canonical failure is about asymmetry, not ignorance. At Sochi 2021 Mercedes read the weather radar, sector times and driver feedback, expected the rain to increase, and pitted Hamilton on lap 49 ([RacingNews365](https://racingnews365.com/mercedes-explain-their-race-winning-russian-gp-strategy-call)). McLaren expected the rain to hold. Norris stayed on slicks "even as lap times slowed by over 10s", slid off, and finished 7th after leading. Seidl called it "about 50/50" and "in hindsight the wrong decision" ([Formula1.com](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/mclaren-didnt-get-it-together-as-a-team-says-seidl-after-norris-misses-out.21pEf2fFvoBjmfNjyMfaew)), and McLaren's review asked why the pit wall had not overruled the driver ([The Race](https://www.the-race.com/formula-1/mclaren-needs-to-work-out-why-it-didnt-overrule-norris)). The costs are lopsided. Pitting early costs a bounded pit loss, about 11 s less under a Safety Car ([F1.com](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/monday-morning-debrief-why-nailing-the-strategy-was-so-tough-at-the-rain.6mVFnAE6LiMq5jK9IjN0gy), via search summary). Inters on a dry track cost **5-6 s a lap** ([Pirelli](https://www.pirelli.com/global/en-ww/race/when-it-s-time-to-change-from-slick-to-wet-tyres-in-formula-1)). Slicks on a worsening wet track cost the race. Formal tools turn this into distributions. TUM's Monte Carlo race simulation scores user-prescribed strategies by thousands of runs and the resulting spread of finishing positions ([MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2076-3417/10/21/7805); abstract only, full text returned 403). Learned and sequential choosers exist too: TUM's Virtual Strategy Engineer uses one network for whether to pit and another for the compound ([TUM](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/virtual-strategy-engineer-using-artificial-neural-networks-for-ma/)), and MCTS has been applied to pit and compound decisions ([PRL 2021](https://prl-theworkshop.github.io/prl2021/papers/PRL2021_paper_1.pdf)). The notes found no published team threshold for rain probability or radar lead time, and no published hysteresis band. The dead band described below is inferred from practice: a team changes tyres only once the delta clearly exceeds the pit loss.

## Rally codes the road ahead and keeps the fast choice cheap

Rally formalises the road ahead as a discrete feature vector, the pace note. It gives direction, an ordinal severity (1-5 or 1-6 depending on the system, from near-straight to hairpin), and modifiers: "+" for tightens, "don't cut" for a hazard on the inside, a caution mark, narrowing lines, and remarks on surface and condition ([Wikipedia: Pacenotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacenotes)). "Right 4 tightens" warns that the corner may close to a 3 or a 2 ([Race and Rally](https://raceandrally.com/blogs/all-blog-posts/rally-pacenotes-explained); secondary source, conventions approximate). Rally separates two time scales. Tyre choice is made hours before a loop, from forecast and from the reports of Monte Carlo "ice/snow note" crews who drive the route shortly before the stage ([Racecar.com](https://www.racecar.com/news/87810/motorsport/citroen-c3-wrc-returns-to-the-monte-carlo-rally); search paraphrase, not fetched). A wrong choice costs about **19 s/km** between slicks and studs on ice ([PMW](https://pmw-magazine.com/features/tire-choice-conundrums.html); **unverified**, from a search summary). Gravel compound rules are keyed on conditions: soft for cool, damp or sandy roads, hard for warm, dry or abrasive ones ([Pirelli, Portugal](https://press.pirelli.com/portuguese-premiere-for-pirellis-scorpion-kx-wrc-tyres/)). Technique is the fast, cheap and reversible choice, called a few hundred metres ahead. The surface class itself goes stale. On dry gravel, cars early in the running order sweep a line for those behind, while after rain the later cars meet mud ([FIA](https://www.fia.com/news/wrc-portugal-primed-seven-event-gravel-stretch-begins); via search summary). The same corner on the same day therefore belongs to different classes for different cars.

Technique follows surface and corner, in practice and in optimal control. Tavernini, Massaro, Velenis et al. find the minimum-time driver "circuit-style, low-drift" on dry and wet pavement and "rally-style, high-drift" on loose surfaces, for RWD, FWD and AWD alike ([Padova IRIS](https://research.unipd.it/handle/11577/2835923); abstract only, **no numbers verified**). Handbrake cornering comes out as the minimum-time *and* minimum-lateral-space strategy on a tight hairpin on loose gravel, validated against an expert driver ([Southampton 382093](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/382093); abstract). Velenis and Tsiotras derive trail braking as a special case of minimum-time cornering ([SAE 2008](https://dcsl.gatech.edu/papers/sae08.pdf)). Coaches agree that on tarmac the fastest technique keeps the tyre at or just below peak slip ([NASA Speed News](https://nasaspeed.news/columns/driver-instruction/detecting-over-driving/)). The Scandinavian flick survives mostly as a loose-surface tool, which a rally-school instructor says "really isn't used much anymore" ([Jalopnik](https://jalopnik.com/learning-the-scandinavian-flick-at-rally-school-5758087)). This is the same structure our study found in `autoware-drift/docs/drift-optimality.md`. On swb's asphalt a drift pays only in hairpins, by 0.4 % at R 9 m, 0.2 % at 15 m, 0.1 % at 25 m and nothing on any 90 deg corner. On low friction (mu 0.3-0.45) it pays in nearly every corner, by 1-2 %. The surface's fall-off past the peak, not its peak alone, decides it on high friction. The literature supports the shape of M3b's table but gives no telemetry that would check its magnitudes.

## What practice teaches the seed scheduler

The motorsport evidence lines up with what M1 and M3 measured, and it makes the case for the reframing after M3 more than for any lap-time chooser. Each lesson maps onto our planner as follows.

| practice | what it decides on | our analogue | consequence for M3b |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pirelli's 110-112 % crossover | measured performance against a reference, not water depth | the drift-optimality map's predicted gain, not mu alone | key the table on predicted gain by (peak mu, fall-off, turn, R, grade); M3b already does |
| Suzuka probe lap | a live sample of the other option near the tie | multi-start: solve G and D both | near the crossover, run both; that *is* the insurance (M3: G and D agree in 71-87 % of re-plans) |
| Sochi 2021 | asymmetric cost, a missing override | missing D on low mu: 1-2 % plus no fallback when G fails (18 % of re-plans at mu 0.3); missing D on asphalt: at most 0.4 % | add D whenever low friction is *plausible*; only a *confident* dry open road gets G alone |
| pit loss as a dead band | switch only once the delta exceeds the switching cost | M3's gate: 0.3 %, two consecutive re-plans, grip on a tie, keep the mode with no corner ahead | adding a seed and committing to its plan are separate decisions with separate thresholds |
| rally tyres vs technique | slow choice early and conservative, fast choice cheap | seeds are reversible each re-plan; a committed slide is not (TUM: 1.1 m lateral error in a held slide) | be generous with seeds and strict with commitment |
| pace note | direction, severity, tightens, don't cut, narrows, surface | turn, *least* radius, distance ahead, width, class | "tightens" means least radius over the corner, not mean; "narrows + hairpin" is where the handbrake literature finds lateral space binding, so the width arm comes before any width rule |
| ice crews, running order | the map's class goes stale | map class with an age and a confidence | low confidence adds seeds, never removes them |
| pit wall vs driver | the global prior can be overruled by local feel | map class (pit wall) vs the ECU's estimate (driver) | a live low-mu estimate may force D seeds and lower mu; it may never raise mu during a slide (060 M6's x0.7 crash) |

Two points sharpen this. First, M3 showed the chooser itself is worth little for lap time: the gate decided 3 times in 18 runs, and course time against the single start ranged from -0.59 % to +0.88 % with no ordering by threshold. On Haruna with mu 0.3 patches, every multi-start run finished where the single start stopped at 10 s. That is the Sochi lesson in our units. The value of the extra option lies in not being caught on the wrong tyre when conditions turn, not in the expected-value gain on a normal lap. M3b's scheduler should therefore be judged by how rarely it *omits* a seed that would have rescued a re-plan, not by how many solves it saves on dry asphalt. Second, the F1 crossover is ambiguous by definition near the tie, and our solver noise sits at the same size as asphalt's gain (live p75 noise 0.04 % on asphalt, 0.30 % at mu 0.45 against gains of 0.1-0.4 % on asphalt hairpins). Asphalt "+ D" rows should therefore require the predicted gain to clear the noise band, not merely be positive. The same holds for the flick, which wins only the R 9 m, no-fall-off asphalt hairpin, and only by 0.05 s.

## No onboard sensor sees friction 200 m ahead

The planner needs peak mu and fall-off per node over 200 m (Haruna) to 260 m (Tsukuba). At Haruna's 36 m/s that is 5-7 s ahead. The sensing literature divides into three groups, and none of them covers the window.

**Effect-based estimators** read the tyre's response, so they see the peak only while the tyre works near it. Slip-based methods need roughly 50 % or more of the grip used, pneumatic-trail and aligning-torque methods 20-50 %, and in-tyre accelerometers about 60 % ([low-excitation review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321344788_Road_Friction_Virtual_Sensing_A_Review_of_Estimation_Techniques_with_Emphasis_on_Low_Excitation_Approaches), abstract/snippet only; [PoliMi](https://www.politesi.polimi.it/handle/10589/109723), snippet only). Once excited they converge fast. A recursive estimator reached a simulated sudden friction drop in about **0.06 s** ([Chalmers](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/241863), snippet only). Their look-ahead, however, is **0 m** by construction. Han, Lee and Choi show that pneumatic-trail stiffness detects the peak "in low slip regions", with less excitation than conventional methods ([ACC 2016](https://acl.kaist.ac.kr/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/2016ACC_HKS.pdf), verified in text, abstract). This is the only effect-based route that warns before the peak, and it helps on the straights and gentle corners leading into a hairpin rather than in the hairpin itself. Svensson and Törngren sum the class up as "highly accurate but only available under slip" ([arXiv 2105.06692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.06692)). This is 060 M6 in one line. A 20 m patch at 100-130 km/h lasts about 0.5-0.7 s, and the braking point for the corner it guards has already passed when the first useful sample arrives. The x0.7 crash also matches a known failure mode: losing or changing excitation makes the estimator fall back to its prior, which was the map's value, while the car was still sliding. An estimator used here must hold the reduced value, with a slow-release forgetting factor, until the patch is confirmed exited.

**Cause-based sensors** do look ahead, but only a short way. Published camera datasets stop at 30 m. RoadSaW labels bird's-eye patches at **7.5, 15, 22.5 and 30 m**, with wetness taken from a Lufft MARWIS's measured film height, and its authors report limits on generalisation ([RoadSaW, CVPRW 2022](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022W/WAD/papers/Cordes_RoadSaW_A_Large-Scale_Dataset_for_Camera-Based_Road_Surface_and_Wetness_CVPRW_2022_paper.pdf)). Class accuracy on benchmarks is about 90-97 %: **94.84 % top-1** on five friction classes on RSCD ([Data in Brief 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352340922006771), snippet only), 92.52 % on the full RSCD for RoadFormer ([arXiv 2506.02358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02358), snippet only), and Volvo's two-stage front camera at **97 %** for surface condition and **89 %** for a high/medium/low friction grade over a patch grid of the ego lane ([Chalmers 510219](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/510219), repository abstract). Accuracies are not comparable across papers because the label sets differ. The best-validated non-contact friction instrument is a spectroscopic NIR sensor. Teconer's RCM411 showed an overall **SD of 0.10 mu** against a braking friction meter over 248 reference points from about 2000 km of winter driving (0.05 in snow, 0.14 on one drive). It looks at the road roughly a metre from the vehicle ([PIARC 2014](https://proceedings-andorra2014.piarc.org/content/actes/13_eng.pdf), verified in text; vendor co-authored). Lidar intensity as a wet, snow or ice cue rests on patent examples, such as flagging a wet road when 85 % or more of road points return intensity below 10 on a 0-250 scale ([US 9207323](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9207323)), not on validated accuracies. For radar and thermal cameras the notes found no quantitative source. The inference that follows is ours, since no source tested ranges beyond 30 m. At 130 km/h, 30 m is 0.8 s, which is shorter than the roughly 52 m needed to brake from 130 to 60 km/h at 1 g. Beyond about 50-80 m a camera can at most separate large, contrasting regions, such as snow cover, standing-water sheen or a change from asphalt to gravel, and it cannot see a slick 20 m asphalt patch. Even where a camera does see, it yields a *class*, and wet asphalt alone spans about 0.4-0.8 mu. **Fall-off past the peak is not measured by any non-contact sensor found.**

**Racing practice** works around both limits with memory and margin rather than preview. The Mainfranken Racing team infers a per-position grip-scaling factor from stability metrics, stores it in a sparse GP that is rebuilt after each lap, and cut lap time from 37.15 s on lap 1 to 24.32 s on lap 10. Over ten laps the mean was 8 % below a non-adaptive baseline ([arXiv 2606.10733](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.10733), verified in text). TUM states that friction "cannot be measured directly" and builds a friction map over repeated laps ([TUM FTM](https://mos.ed.tum.de/en/ftm/main-research/intelligent-vehicle-systems/estimation-and-prediction-of-the-tire-road-friction-potential-of-an-autonomous-racecar)). TUM's IAC car reportedly ran its gg-diagram at 0.8 on the warm-up lap, 0.9 on lap 2 and **0.925** on the timed lap ([JFR 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rob.22153); search summary, exact page not confirmed). Our live grip share g1 = 0.92 sits at that timed-lap factor. Like the 0.8 warm-up factor, it absorbs a x0.8 patch but not x0.7, which is exactly what the model loop showed. GripMap adds spatially resolved constraints for planning, and a snippet ties it to a 2023 IAC low-grip incident ([arXiv 2504.12115](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.12115); incident unverified). The established fusion pattern uses the class as a prior and the dynamics estimate as the measurement update. Svensson and Törngren's heteroscedastic GP over space, with cheap and noisy camera points and accurate but sparse slip points, is the form closest to a planner's needs ([arXiv 2105.06692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.06692), simulation only, no numbers extracted). A Chalmers thesis uses a Kalman filter that forgets toward the classified surface's prior ([Chalmers](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/127242.pdf), snippet only). No fused system in the notes reports quantified look-ahead gained.

## The map carries surface, geometry and grade, with known errors

Off-board sources give priors and event flags, not per-node values. Road-weather stations are points kilometres apart: about 775 in Sweden ([Trafikverket](https://bransch.trafikverket.se/contentassets/28744f2661f44c22b63b4042092fadfd/ersattningsmodell_vaders_2019.pdf)) and over 350 in Finland, updated each minute ([Digitraffic](https://www.digitraffic.fi/en/road-traffic/)). Their optical "grip" index correlates poorly with measured friction at low mu ([Montana State](https://scholarworks.montana.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bd414ee0-f7a4-49b7-b73d-580fb18b6539/content); from summaries, verify before quoting). Connected-car services deliver slippery-road *events*, not mu profiles. TomTom states a round-trip latency under **5 s**, with creation and expiry time on every message ([TomTom](https://download.tomtom.com/open/banners/White-paper-TomTom-Hazard-Warnings.pdf), vendor claim). Bosch's NIRA alerts are said to be map-matched to under 10 m ([motorsactu](https://motorsactu.com/securite-routiere-bosch-integre-les-road-surface-alerts-de-nira-dynamics-dans-ses-services-connectes/), unverified). A simulated cooperative estimator needs **at least 50 recently passed vehicles** for about 10 % worst-case error ([arXiv 2302.03560](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03560)). ETSI's DENM carries "adverse weather - adhesion" with sub-causes such as ice, black ice and oil, and a default validity of **600 s** ([ETSI LibIts](https://forge.etsi.org/rep/LIBS/LibIts/-/raw/4a5168b1c95fafc44d81b45648a035bb4a90d3b3/asn1/DENM/DENM.asn)). Two consequences follow. Absence of an event is not evidence of a dry road, especially on a closed course or a mountain pass with no fleet traffic. And a source accurate to about 0.1 mu can choose between M3b's wet (0.6-0.8) and low (≤ 0.45) classes, but cannot supply a number within them.

Map formats carry surface categorically. Lanelet2's only surface attribute is `road_surface` ([Lanelet2 tagging](https://github.com/fzi-forschungszentrum-informatik/Lanelet2/blob/master/lanelet2_core/doc/LaneletAndAreaTagging.md)). OSM has `surface=*` and `smoothness=*` ([OSM wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Key:smoothness)). OpenDRIVE is the one open format with a numeric per-lane `material@friction`, optionally overridden by an OpenCRG friction grid ([ASAM OpenDRIVE](https://publications.pages.asam.net/standards/ASAM_OpenDRIVE/ASAM_OpenDRIVE_Specification/latest/specification/11_lanes/11_07_lane_properties.html); content from search summary, page 404). **None carries fall-off.** A planner must therefore map the category to its own class table, which is what swb's `config/surface_classes.yaml` already is. It gives per class a peak `mu`, `wet` factors per OpenSCENARIO wetness level, `peak_slip`, `peak_slip_angle`, `sliding` (the share kept past the peak), `relaxation` and an optional Stribeck term. The values come from the literature's ranges, "not measured". The course's own `surfaces.yaml` (Haruna) today holds only label defaults with `zones: []`.

Geometry is where real maps and our sim diverge. OSM centrelines sit **2-6 m** from survey references: a mean of 2.3 m and over 86 % of segments within 5 m in Calgary ([ICA 2023](https://ica-abs.copernicus.org/articles/6/124/2023/ica-abs-6-124-2023.pdf)), and 95 % within 8.5-13 m in three African cities ([ISPRS 2016](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLI-B2/615/2016/isprs-archives-XLI-B2-615-2016.pdf)). On an R 9-25 m hairpin that offset is 10-60 % of the radius. OSM therefore reliably says that a corner of 150 deg or more is coming, but it cannot place the corner on the right side of M3b's R 9 m and R 25 m thresholds. SRTM's vertical error of **±16 m** ([FIG 2022](https://fig.net/resources/proceedings/fig_proceedings/fig2022/papers/ts04c/TS04C_musa_naiya_et_al_11646.pdf)) is larger than the 10 m drop that defines M3b's "> 5 % downhill" over 200 m. Open DEMs are known to be poor for incline on short segments ([Heidelberg GIScience](https://giscienceblog.uni-heidelberg.de/?p=5449)). Width is sparsely tagged, and the notes found no completeness statistics. In Isaac none of this is an error: the course generator builds the world mesh from the same OSM and DEM data the planner reads, so geometry, grade and width ahead are exact *with respect to the sim world*. They become uncertain only when we model a real road, and then a surveyed HD map or a national LiDAR DEM is required for the radius and grade thresholds.

## Recommendation for M3b

Our requirements put the preconditions into the table below. Effect-based estimators, camera classifiers and fleet services all fall short of the 200-260 m window, so the map is the only source that covers it.

| precondition | prior, over 0-260 m | confirmation, 0-30 (60) m | correction, after the car has driven it | accuracy to plan with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| surface class (peak mu, fall-off, peak slip angle) | `surfaces.yaml` zones mapped through `surface_classes.yaml`, with a per-zone confidence and age; wetness shifts the whole window | a simulated perception sensor: true class at distance d through a distance-dependent confusion matrix | the ECU's slip and aligning-torque estimate, written back to the map for the next pass, hold-low on release | class probabilities; mu at a lower quantile, about ±0.1 SD per class; fall-off only from the class |
| turn, least radius, distance | course centreline (exact in sim) | none needed in sim | none | exact in sim; on real OSM, widen the R thresholds by the map's 2-6 m |
| grade | course mesh (exact in sim) | none | none | exact in sim; SRTM inadequate for 5 % over 200 m |
| width | course (exact in sim) | none | none | unknown means the narrowest arm |

**Sources.** The prior is the map. Each node's surface is a set of class probabilities taken from its `surfaces.yaml` zone. A zone authored from survey or from previous passes gets high confidence, and a zone inferred from an OSM `surface` tag or left at a label default gets lower confidence. A weather or wetness level shifts the whole window's distribution, which is the role RWIS and race weather services play in practice. The confirmation is a perception channel covering only the near window. The timing has a consequence the table does not show. At 30-36 m/s the 30-60 m a camera covers arrives in the last one or two re-plans before M3b's "no new D under ~25 m" cut-off. Perception therefore cannot *add* a seed in time for the corner it sees. It can only veto a commitment, lower the planned mu, and feed L2. Seeds come from the prior. The correction is the ECU's estimator, used downgrade-only while live: it may lower mu and force D seeds for the next corners, but never raise mu during a slide. Its upward updates are gated on stable, non-saturated driving and on the map or perception saying the patch has ended. Its main use is to rewrite the map's zone after a pass, as racing teams do lap to lap. Fixing its exit, which 060 M6 already names, comes before it is switched on.

**Carrying uncertainty.** Each node carries class probabilities p(c), not a mu value. L1's friction constraint uses a lower quantile, mu_q = mu_mean − k·sigma, computed over the class mixture. An unconfirmed patch then costs time rather than a crash, which is how M6's "uncertainty planned in" enters the cost directly. The quantile does not replace g1 = 0.92, which 060 ruled is not a friction margin. Fall-off is taken per class and never from a sensor. Fusion is asymmetric, following the notes' design suggestion. A slippery report downgrades at once. Upgrading back to the drier class needs onboard evidence or the report's expiry, with the 600 s DENM default as a ready-made staleness bound for injected hazards.

**The seed rule under uncertainty: add loosely, commit strictly.** The *add* rule takes the union of M3b's table rows over every class whose probability exceeds a low threshold p_add. A window that is 15 % likely to be snow gets the snow row's seeds (G + D, + F into hairpins). The *commit* rule is M3's existing gate (0.3 % of the window's time, two consecutive re-plans, grip on a tie), with two additions. On asphalt rows the predicted gain must clear the noise band. A drift plan is adopted only if it still wins when both plans are scored at the window's lower-quantile mu, so a plan that wins only on the optimistic class is not adopted. Where the class choice is uncertain, the two rules pull in opposite directions on purpose. For seeding, assume the class that makes a drift pay: unknown fall-off is seeded as no fall-off, because there drift is optimal on every corner of asphalt-level mu. For the constraint, assume the class with the lowest mu. G always runs. Seeds already on the corner library's archetypes keep their plans across re-plans. Seeding is decided in the window's far part (≥ 25 m to the corner) and is never decided by perception. The expected-value form from the motorsport notes sets the scale of p_add: E[loss of omitting D] = P(low or wet | window) × (1-2 % plus the fallback when G fails, 18 % of re-plans at mu 0.3), against a core and up to 0.9 s of budget. With the asphalt worst case at 0.4 %, p_add belongs near 0.1-0.2. That number is a starting point for M4 to sweep, not a measured result. Unknown width takes the narrowest arm until the width arm (6, 8, 10, 15 m) has produced a rule.

**What to build and test in sim.** Because swb owns the ground truth, the experiment can separate the map from the world. First, give `surfaces.yaml` zones a *declared* class, which the planner reads, and a *true* class, which the world server applies, and inject map error. The cases are a dry map over a wet or snow zone, a snow map over a cleared zone, and zones made stale over time. Second, build a virtual perception sensor that reads the true class at distance d and flips it through a confusion matrix whose accuracy falls with distance: about 95 % at 30 m or less, near chance by about 100 m for 20 m patches, and more slowly for large zones. Attach the classes' mu priors with ±0.1 SD. These figures are grounded in RoadSaW's 30 m, the 89-97 % benchmark accuracies and Teconer's 0.10 SD. A real classifier trained on RTX renders would measure Isaac's materials, not the world. It is worth doing only later, and the notes found no study of sim-to-real accuracy for one. Lidar intensity is a cheap second channel only if the classes get distinct NIR reflectances, and it has no literature accuracy to calibrate against. Third, give the ECU estimator its hold-low exit. M4's arms then gain a fourth: seeds by precondition *with* the uncertainty layer, run against map error rates of 0, 10 and 30 %, patch lengths of 20, 60 and 200 m, and perception on or off. All are run at mu 1.03, 0.45 and 0.3 on the R 9/15/25 corners and Haruna. Each run is scored per corner against the oracle, with stops and failed re-plans first and solves per re-plan second. A reasonable gate is that the scheduled arm, with 10 % map error, stops no more often than always-on {G, D} and solves fewer seeds on dry asphalt. If it misses that gate, always-on multi-start on dedicated cores is the answer, and the scheduler only orders seeds.

**What stays out of scope.** The unseen 20 m low-grip patch met at the limit on first contact stays out (060 M6, 061's non-goal). No sensor in the notes sees it in time, and practice handles it with margins and memory. The lower-quantile mu is the only lever M3b adds for it. Also out of scope: training or validating a real camera classifier, real off-board feeds (RWIS, DENM, NIRA, HERE, TomTom) beyond a whole-window weather stub, sensing fall-off, any width rule before the width arm, M7's learned chooser (which would take these same inputs), and geometry error, which is zero in our worlds and belongs to a real-road phase.

## Conclusion

Motorsport and our measurements agree that the value of the drift seed is robustness, not speed. The teams that lost in the notes did not lack a forecast. They kept too few options open while the precondition got worse, and had no channel for a pessimistic local reading to override the plan. The sensing literature narrows the design further than the roadmap assumed. Seed choice can only ever be driven by the map: perception arrives inside the last re-plan or two before the D cut-off, and the car's own estimate only after the slide has begun. The sim's real job is therefore not perception fidelity. It is to measure how the planner behaves when the map is wrong.

Two open numbers decide whether M3b's scheduler is worth keeping over always-on multi-start. The first is the add threshold p_add at which a missed seed stops costing stops. The second is how many solves the schedule saves on dry asphalt once that threshold holds. Neither number appears in any source in the notes, and both are cheap to measure with a declared-against-true surface map and a distance-degraded virtual sensor.
